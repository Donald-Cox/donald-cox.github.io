How this works

Why it exists. The verification system was working. It was reading labels correctly and naming discrepancies correctly. At least four wrong shipments went out anyway. In three of them the discrepancy was raised in a way that could be clicked past, and nothing downstream ever asked whether a flag had been resolved or merely dismissed. On every dashboard those orders were green. They passed.

Shape. A read-only collector runs against the workflow engine's own execution database through a watermark, every five minutes, during working hours. It writes nothing to anything it observes and keeps its own findings store. Findings are triaged by a reasoning model, and the assessment sits beside the raw evidence. The model advises and changes no verdict.

Every rule records the incident that created it. That is a deliberate documentation practice: nobody may delete a rule without reading why it was written. Open the provenance line on any finding above.

Investigation. Click a finding, or type any order number, and get the attempts timeline in local time, who was signed in for each attempt, per line confidence against its floor, every flag raised, and the actual photographs the vision model judged, recovered from the execution record. Before this existed that reconstruction was a series of manual database queries and a hunt through cloud storage folders.

Two traps worth knowing. An order lookup that returns nothing usually means the execution history has aged out of retention, not that the record never existed, so queries are bounded by date. And order numbers appear quoted inside hundreds of unrelated execution payloads, so a plain substring search returns noise; photograph recovery is anchored on the finding's own execution identifiers.

A fault this watcher did not catch. An AI component feeding its training queue returned nothing for eighteen hours while re-billing the same three calls every thirty minutes. It looked healthy from outside. The morning check and an audit found it. The cause was a token budget that the model's own default reasoning consumed entirely, leaving no room for output. Repairing it surfaced two wrong-shipment risks that had been invisible, and the failure now has its own rule.

It runs on the server, not on a desk. This started as a compiled desktop program and that version was retired, because the floor has to be watched even when a particular workstation is off. On the day that decision was made, the machinist's workstation was asleep for the entire build session and nothing was blocked by it. Each desk now opens the same URL as a desktop-app-mode window, and rebuilding the container updates every desk at once.

Plain text for operators. Model prose is normalized before it reaches an operator. Characters are never deleted, because deleting welds neighbors together and silently corrupts arithmetic inside a note.

Measured. Sweep every five minutes, working hours, weekdays, for known failure classes. It makes no promise about every defect. Calibrated over a 197 execution window. Zero blunt hard-failure-but-passed cases found. One candidate rule retired after 215 false fires.
